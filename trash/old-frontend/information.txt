VERDICT — General Concept
What VERDICT is
VERDICT is a working full-stack system, built by a team of three led by Toko, that helps hiring organizations cut through hundreds of CVs and find the candidates who are actually the best — not the ones who simply write the most confident applications. Recruiters are often forced to skim, and selection ends up closer to random than it should be. Worse, some applicants exaggerate or outright lie on their CVs just to reach the interview or land an offer. VERDICT exists to take that power back and put it in the organizer's hands: every claim a candidate makes is checked against evidence before it is trusted.
The core principle is simple — confidence is not evidence. A CV can say "expert," "led a team," "deep experience in X," but those words mean nothing until something backs them up. VERDICT treats a CV as a set of claims to be cross-examined, scores how well each one survives that examination, and turns a stack of polished documents into a ranked, inspectable, evidence-backed view of who the real candidates are.
When applicants submit their CVs, they agree to VERDICT's terms of service and consent to their personal information being stored and processed as required by Georgian law. That consent is what authorizes the deeper verification described below.
The four States
VERDICT runs audits through selectable "States," each a different level of strictness and intelligence. They escalate from fast and deterministic to deep and adversarial, so an organizer can match the level of scrutiny to the stakes of the role.
Light State uses no AI. It relies on a large, admin-maintained template of catchphrases and rules to catch only the things that are obviously false or self-contradicting from the CV alone — the low-effort lies that don't even survive a careful read. It's fast, cheap, and good for a first pass over a large pile.
WAR State also runs on templates but is strict and uncompromising. It flags claims that the CV itself fails to justify — someone says they have deep knowledge of a technology, but there's no project, role, or experience anywhere in the document that supports it. WAR State doesn't give the benefit of the doubt; if the claim isn't backed, it's marked.
Person State introduces AI and reads more like a sharp human reviewer would. It judges the impression a CV creates — whether a line reads as a genuine, substantiated skill or just another nice-sounding technical phrase — and uses AI to tell the difference between real signal and surface polish.
WAR+AI State is the strictest and most powerful. It demands backing for everything and combines deterministic rules with AI judgment. This is also where OSINT can be switched on. The result is a full, detailed report: claims that are truly backed (a "deep experience in X" supported by a real GitHub project, with the link attached; a role confirmed by a LinkedIn company and title) are validated, and claims that aren't are exposed for what they are.
OSINT and the Investigator capability
OSINT verification is strongest inside WAR+AI, but it's best understood as a capability rather than a single setting. In WAR+AI it acts as the deepest verification layer; it can also be treated as a standalone investigator escalation that any audit can be pushed into when an organizer wants to go beyond the documents.
With the candidate's consent, the OSINT system takes the name and the details supplied in the job application and searches for that person across LinkedIn, Instagram, Facebook, and GitHub. It gathers what it finds, and for technical candidates it can look at the actual projects — what the person used, what they built, the substance of the work — and even estimate how heavily their code relies on AI. The system compiles everything into a structured report file and feeds it back into the score, so an external project link or a confirmed employer becomes real corroboration attached to the specific claim it supports.
Candidate memory
A key differentiator: VERDICT remembers. The system stores candidate information over time, so if an applicant was flagged a year ago and submits a fresh CV today, VERDICT recognizes them and brings that history into the new review. An organization doesn't start from zero on every application — it builds an accumulating memory of who it has seen and how they checked out, which makes repeat exaggeration far harder to slip through.
Templates
The States run on templates, managed as large JSON structures full of catchphrases, patterns, and rules that decide what matters and what should be ignored (contact details, profile links, harmless metadata). The big, official templates are built and maintained by verified admins — us — and receive ongoing updates. Alongside these there's a community layer, so users can create and share their own, while the verified admin templates remain the trusted, continuously improved backbone.
Scoring and the end report
Everything VERDICT does accrues into a visible score. Each verified claim adds points; each unsupported, stretched, or contradicted one costs them. The OSINT layer scores continuously too — and crucially, absence counts: a private GitHub, a hidden LinkedIn, or a candidate who simply isn't showing something costs points, because hidden evidence is not the same as confirmed evidence. All of this rolls up into a final credibility score presented as a clear chart in the end-report analysis, so an organizer sees at a glance not just a number but where it came from — which claims held up, which didn't, and what the candidate chose not to reveal.
Visual identity
VERDICT's look is deliberately stoic and minimal. The intent is to feel strong, secure, and ten steps ahead — the same confidence a serious game anticheat projects, where the interface itself signals that the system knows exactly what it's doing and has full command of the situation. No clutter, no noise: a calm, authoritative surface that makes the user trust the verdict behind it. The minimalism is the message — the tool is sure of itself, so the user can be too.
Technical foundation
VERDICT is already built and functioning on a Spring Boot backend, a React frontend, and MySQL. The database persists user accounts, candidate records (including the historical reputation memory that lets it recognize returning applicants), audit results and their lifecycle, and the audit templates — stored with fields like template name, author, visibility, and the large catchphrase string that drives detection — along with the logs, scores, and report data the rest of the system depends on.

# VERDICT Project Description

VERDICT is a full-stack web application created for BrainHack_AI 2026 as an AI-powered claim audit system. Its main idea is simple but strong: instead of using AI to generate more polished text, VERDICT uses AI and deterministic analysis to question whether existing text is actually supported by evidence. The product works like a cross-examiner. A user provides a claim document, such as a CV, sales page, pitch deck, or report, and an evidence document, such as a job posting, source document, product specification, reference notes, or dataset. VERDICT then checks the claims line by line and returns a clear judgment.

The reason for creating VERDICT is that modern documents often sound confident even when the proof behind them is weak. People write “expert,” “proven,” “optimized,” “best-in-class,” “production-ready,” or “led a team,” but those phrases are not always backed by real evidence. This project was built to expose that gap. Its core message is that confidence is not evidence. A claim should survive comparison with proof before it is trusted.

The website currently supports several audit modes: CV versus job posting, sales claim versus source documents, pitch deck versus evidence, and report versus references. Each mode has its own labels, templates, sample demo cases, and review behavior. Users can choose official templates, create private templates, or use shared templates. Templates decide what should be treated as important and what should be ignored, such as contact details, profile links, headings, or harmless metadata. The system also includes different check states, including WAR-State, Light-State, Person-State, and Strict WAR+AI-State, allowing users to choose between fast deterministic review, lenient checking, human-like contextual review, or strict high-stakes auditing.

VERDICT includes a private logged-in dashboard where users can see recent audits, average credibility score, risky claim counts, saved reports, and audit history. Each audit has a detailed result page with score, status lifecycle, timestamps, analyzer and prompt version, evidence panels, verdict filters, suspicious word detection, safer rewrite suggestions, dense result tables, sticky summary bars, and export controls. Reports can be exported as TXT, Word-compatible DOC, Excel-compatible CSV, or presentation-ready PDF.

A newer part of the project is the profiling mode. This mode presents the original document as a reading surface and highlights important words or phrases. Hovering or clicking on a phrase explains its role, risk level, safer wording, evidence pressure, and relationship to the selected template or check state. This makes the tool feel closer to a serious document inspector than a simple result table.

Technically, the project uses a Spring Boot backend with JPA, authentication, admin tools, async audit jobs, request tracing, structured errors, soft delete, indexes, service-layer tests, and export generation. The frontend is built with React and Vite, with separate components, pages, hooks, API helpers, dashboard views, profile management, admin search, mobile polish, skeleton loaders, and demo flows.

In the future, VERDICT could add score levels out of 10, stronger analytics, team workspaces, better template marketplaces, and an investigator mode that checks public sources such as LinkedIn, GitHub, certificates, portfolios, publications, and other open evidence. Overall, VERDICT is valuable because it saves time, improves trust, reduces exaggeration, and turns vague professional claims into inspectable, scored, evidence-backed reports.

OSINT Verification Engine — Technical Plan
Purpose
An open-source-intelligence (OSINT) backend that analyzes and verifies the claims made by job applicants in their CVs. Every candidate explicitly consents to the processing and storage of their personal data in accordance with Georgian privacy law before any verification runs. This consent authorizes us to search public sources and corroborate the statements in their CV — for example, when a claimed job title or tenure is in question.
The system targets material misrepresentations — fabricated roles, employers a person never worked for, or experience that cannot be corroborated anywhere — not the minor discrepancies (rounded dates, reworded titles) that are normal and expected.
Identity resolution: multi-signal anchoring
Identity is established before any claim is scored, because every downstream signal depends on confirming we're looking at the right person. Rather than trusting any single identifier, the system anchors identity on a combination of signals — email, name, claimed employer, and location — and only treats a match as valid when those signals corroborate one another.
This directly addresses the two failure modes that break naïve verification: common names (many people share "David Kvaratskhelia") and missing emails (the address never surfaces publicly). A name appearing on a company site is only accepted as the candidate when it co-occurs with at least one other anchor — a matching email domain, a corroborating role, or a consistent location. When signals conflict or are too sparse to anchor confidently, the case is flagged for human review rather than guessed.
Data sourcing: APIs and licensed sources first
The system prefers official APIs and licensed data over scraping, for stability and reliability:
LinkedIn API — candidate and company data, used as an independent cross-reference against the CV.
GitHub API — developer signals for technical roles (see below).
Search / enrichment APIs — to locate where an email or name appears across the public web.
Licensed people-data providers — structured corroboration where available.
Direct scraping of public pages is treated as a last resort, used only to read a specific public page (e.g., a company team page) when no API exposes that data. This keeps the pipeline maintainable and avoids dependence on fragile scrapers as the primary engine.
Verification logic
We begin from the most reliable anchor: the candidate's email. We search for where that email appears and what each site represents, while in parallel checking the candidate's name against the official sites of listed employers.
A mention on a company's own site is a strong signal — but a mention alone is not full verification. The system treats two things as separate steps, with the second weighted more heavily:
Existence — this person is associated with this company.
Specific claim — this person held this role during this timeframe.
Scoring: weighted per-claim, rolled into overall confidence
Each claim on the CV is scored independently. Evidence contributes weighted points:
Email appears on a credible, identifiable website → base weight.
That site is tied to a listed employer or job listing → added weight.
The site confirms the specific role claimed → higher weight.
The site confirms the timeframe → further weight.
Contradicting evidence → negative weight.
Per-claim scores roll up into an overall CV confidence score, and every score carries its reasons — the specific evidence that produced it. The output tells a reviewer not just how confident the system is, but which parts of the CV are well-supported and why, so weak or unsupported claims are visible at a glance rather than buried in a single number.
Technical / engineering roles
For technical positions, verification extends to developer signals via the GitHub API: the languages the candidate actually uses, the count of public repositories available for review, and the substance of that public work — converting "claims to code in X" into observable evidence that feeds the relevant claim's score.
Direct verification: automated, with human review on uncertainty
Where public sources are inconclusive, the system automatically generates and sends verification inquiries to listed employers via email — using templated requests sent only to verified company domains, with every request and response tracked in an audit log. Sending is fully automated by default.
When a case carries uncertainty — ambiguous identity, conflicting evidence, or a high-stakes contradiction — it is routed to a human reviewer who can approve, edit, or hold the inquiry before it goes out. This keeps the common path fast and hands-off while keeping a person in the loop exactly where judgment matters. Company responses feed back into the relevant claim's score.

OSINT Verification Engine — Implementation Plan
Guiding principle
Build the pipeline as a sequence of independent, idempotent stages connected by a queue, where each stage reads from and writes to a persistent VerificationCase record. Every stage emits evidence (immutable, append-only) and the score is derived from evidence, never stored as a bare number. This makes the system resumable (for human-review pauses and slow APIs), auditable (Georgian privacy compliance), and explainable (every score carries its reasons).

Phase 0 — Foundations & compliance gate
This phase is non-negotiable and comes first because consent is a legal precondition, not a feature.
0.1 Project scaffold. FastAPI app, Poetry/uv for deps, Ruff + mypy, pytest, Docker Compose with Postgres + Redis. Settings via pydantic-settings (all secrets via env, never committed).
0.2 Consent & data-protection layer. A Consent model recording: candidate ID, consent text version, timestamp, scope (which sources authorized), legal basis, and expiry. No stage may run without a valid, unexpired consent row — enforce this as a hard precondition checked at job enqueue time, not deep in the pipeline. Add a data-retention policy (TTL on personal data), a right-to-erasure endpoint (cascades delete across cases/evidence/audit), and field-level encryption for PII at rest.
0.3 Audit log. Append-only AuditEvent table (who/what/when/why) written by every stage and every outbound action. This is both a compliance artifact and your debugging backbone.
Deliverable: running skeleton, migrations, consent enforced, audit log live.

Phase 1 — Data model & domain core
Define the spine everything else hangs off:
Candidate — name, emails, location, links (PII-encrypted).
CV → Claim[] — each claim normalized: {type: employment|education|skill, employer, role, start, end, raw_text}. CV parsing into claims is its own module (start with structured JSON input; add a parser later).
VerificationCase — one per CV-run, holds status (pending → resolving_identity → gathering → scoring → awaiting_human → awaiting_employer → complete), and the resolved Identity.
Evidence — immutable: {claim_id, source, source_url, signal_type, raw_payload, weight_contribution, polarity (+/−), confidence, observed_at}.
Score — derived view per-claim and per-CV, always recomputed from evidence.
Key decision: evidence is append-only; scores are projections. Re-running a stage adds evidence, never mutates it. This keeps the audit trail clean and makes scoring deterministic and re-runnable.

Phase 2 — Source adapter layer (the integration boundary)
One uniform SourceAdapter interface so the pipeline never knows which vendor it's talking to: search(identity_signals) -> RawSignal[]. Each adapter handles its own auth, rate-limiting (token bucket), retries with backoff, response caching (Redis, TTL'd — also saves money), and graceful degradation when unavailable.
Adapters to build, in order of difficulty/value:
GitHub adapter (build first — free, well-documented, high signal). Languages used, public repo count, contribution substance. Maps to skill/technical claims.
Search/enrichment adapter (core engine). Wrap your chosen provider (SerpAPI/Bing + a people-data provider). Locates where an email or name appears; classifies each hit (company site, job board, social, directory).
Email-presence / domain adapter. Resolves employer → official domain (so "mention on company site" can be verified and so outreach only goes to verified domains).
LinkedIn adapter (via a licensed third-party provider — flag the ToS/legal review now). Independent cross-reference of role/employer/tenure. Treat as one signal, not ground truth.
Direct-page reader (last resort). Fetches one specified public page (e.g. a team page) only when no API exposes it. Sandboxed, rate-limited, robots-aware.
Build every adapter behind a feature flag with a fake/replay implementation so the whole pipeline is testable offline and you don't burn paid API quota in CI.

Phase 3 — Identity resolution (the gate before scoring)
A dedicated stage that runs before any claim is scored, because every downstream signal depends on confirming the right person.
Multi-signal anchoring: collect candidate signals (email, name, claimed employer, location). A name match is only accepted when it co-occurs with at least one independent anchor (matching email domain, corroborating role, or consistent location). Implement as a scoring function over signal-pairs with explicit thresholds:
Strong anchor → proceed to scoring.
Sparse/conflicting signals → set case status awaiting_human, do not guess.
This directly handles the two named failure modes (common names, missing email). Output is a ResolvedIdentity with its own confidence and the evidence that produced it.

Phase 4 — Verification & two-step claim logic
For each claim, gather evidence and separate two distinct questions, weighting the second more heavily:
Existence — person is associated with this company.
Specific claim — held this role during this timeframe.
Pipeline: start from the email anchor (most reliable) → find where it appears and what each site represents → in parallel, check the name against listed employers' official sites → for technical roles, pull GitHub signals → record everything as Evidence with polarity and a provisional weight.

Phase 5 — Scoring engine
Pure, deterministic function: evidence[] -> ClaimScore, then claim_scores[] -> CVConfidence. Weights as configurable, versioned policy (not hardcoded), so you can tune without redeploying logic:
Email on a credible, identifiable site → base weight.
Site tied to listed employer / job listing → added weight.
Site confirms the specific role → higher weight.
Site confirms timeframe → further weight.
Contradicting evidence → negative weight.
Every ClaimScore carries its reason list (the contributing evidence). The CV rollup surfaces which claims are weak — never collapses to a single opaque number. Snapshot the policy version into each score so historical results stay explainable.
Focus on material misrepresentation, not minor discrepancies — encode tolerance bands (e.g. rounded dates, reworded titles don't count as contradictions).

Phase 6 — Employer outreach (automated, human-in-the-loop on uncertainty)
A stage triggered when public evidence is inconclusive:
Templated inquiries, sent only to verified company domains (from Phase 2.3).
Automated by default; cases with ambiguous identity, conflicting evidence, or high-stakes contradiction route to awaiting_human for approve/edit/hold before send.
Outbound via SES/SMTP; inbound response parsing (unique reply-to token per case, IMAP/webhook ingestion) feeds responses back as new Evidence → triggers re-score.
Every request and response written to the audit log.
This is where the queue + resumable-state design pays off: a case can sit in awaiting_employer for days without holding a worker.

Phase 7 — Human review interface (API only)
Backend endpoints (no frontend, as scoped): list cases needing review, fetch a case with all evidence + provisional score + the specific reason it was flagged, and actions (approve / edit / hold / override identity / approve-or-edit outreach). Every action audited. The frontend team consumes these later.

Phase 8 — Orchestration, observability, hardening
Orchestration: Celery/RQ (or Temporal if you want first-class pause/resume + retries — worth considering given the long-lived awaiting_* states). Each stage = a task; the VerificationCase.status is the source of truth.
Observability: structured logging, per-stage metrics, cost tracking per external call, dead-letter queue for failed jobs.
Security: secrets manager, PII encryption, rate limits on public endpoints, RBAC for reviewers.
Testing: unit tests for the scoring function (deterministic — easy to test exhaustively), contract tests per adapter against recorded fixtures, end-to-end test on a synthetic CV through the full state machine.

Suggested build order (dependency-aware)
Phase 0 → 1 (foundations + model) → 2.1 GitHub adapter + 5 scoring engine (prove the evidence→score loop end-to-end on the cheapest source first) → 3 identity → 2.2/2.3 search+domain adapters → 4 verification → 6 outreach → 2.4 LinkedIn → 7 review API → 8 hardening.
The reason for that ordering: get a thin vertical slice (one source → evidence → score → explainable output) working before you add breadth. It de-risks the core abstraction (evidence-derived scoring) before you spend money on paid APIs.
